# Noise Analysis

Noise sets the fundamental sensitivity limit of any analog circuit. Understanding its sources — and how they combine and refer to the input — is essential for designing low-noise amplifiers, ADCs, oscillators, and sensor interfaces. This article covers the three main noise mechanisms in IC design, shows how to calculate and combine them in circuits, and walks through noise simulation in Cadence Virtuoso.

<div id="thermal"></div>

## Thermal Noise

Thermal noise (Johnson-Nyquist noise) is generated by the random thermal motion of charge carriers in any resistive element. It is present in resistors, MOSFETs (via channel resistance), and BJTs (via base resistance).

**Noise spectral density (voltage):**

$$S_V(f) = 4k_BTR \quad [\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}]$$

**RMS noise voltage over bandwidth B:**

$$V_{n,\text{rms}} = \sqrt{4k_BTR \cdot B}$$

where $k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ is temperature in kelvin, $R$ is resistance in ohms, and $B$ is the noise bandwidth in Hz.

Thermal noise is **white** — flat across frequency — and is independent of DC bias. The equivalent circuit model is a noiseless resistor in series with a voltage noise source $\overline{v_n^2} = 4k_BTR$, or equivalently in parallel with a current noise source $\overline{i_n^2} = 4k_BT/R$.

### Worked Example

A 10 kΩ resistor at room temperature ($T = 300$ K) over a 1 MHz bandwidth:

$$V_{n,\text{rms}} = \sqrt{4 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 \times 10^4 \times 10^6} = \sqrt{1.656 \times 10^{-10}} \approx 12.9 \; \mu\text{V}$$

This is the minimum noise floor any circuit using this resistor in its signal path must contend with.

## Flicker Noise (1/f Noise)

Flicker noise arises from carrier trapping and release at defect sites in the gate oxide of MOSFETs. Its power spectral density falls with frequency, making it dominant at low frequencies.

**Noise spectral density (drain current referred):**

$$S_{I_D}(f) = \frac{K \cdot I_D^{\alpha}}{C_{\text{ox}} \cdot W \cdot L \cdot f} \quad [\text{A}^2/\text{Hz}]$$

where $K$ is a process-dependent constant, $C_{\text{ox}}$ is gate oxide capacitance per unit area, $W/L$ is the transistor aspect ratio, and $\alpha \approx 1$–$2$ depending on the model.

**Gate-referred (input-referred) flicker noise:**

$$S_{V_{GS}}(f) = \frac{K}{C_{\text{ox}} \cdot W \cdot L \cdot f} \quad [\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}]$$

Key observations:
- PMOS transistors typically have lower flicker noise than NMOS (fewer interface traps) — prefer PMOS for input pairs in low-noise amplifiers
- Larger $WL$ product reduces flicker noise — use large-area input transistors when flicker noise is a concern
- Flicker noise is independent of bandwidth but depends on the 1/f slope

### Flicker Corner Frequency

The **flicker corner** $f_c$ is the frequency at which flicker noise equals the thermal noise floor of the same device:

$$f_c = \frac{K \cdot g_m}{4k_BT \cdot C_{\text{ox}} \cdot W \cdot L}$$

Above $f_c$, thermal noise dominates. Below $f_c$, flicker noise dominates. Typical CMOS processes have $f_c$ in the range of 1 kHz–10 MHz.

### Worked Example

For an NMOS device with $W/L = 100\,\mu\text{m}/1\,\mu\text{m}$, $C_{\text{ox}} = 8\,\text{fF}/\mu\text{m}^2$, and $K = 10^{-25}\,\text{V}^2\text{F}$:

$$S_{V_{GS}}(1\,\text{kHz}) = \frac{10^{-25}}{8 \times 10^{-15} \times 100 \times 10^{-12} \times 10^3} \approx 1.25 \times 10^{-4} \; \text{nV}^2/\text{Hz}$$

This is significantly larger than the thermal noise of a typical resistor at the same frequency, confirming that flicker noise dominates at low frequencies.

## Shot Noise

Shot noise arises from the discrete, quantized nature of electric charge crossing a potential barrier. It is present in BJTs (collector and base currents), diodes (forward-biased junctions), and photodetectors.

**Noise spectral density (current):**

$$S_I(f) = 2qI \quad [\text{A}^2/\text{Hz}]$$

where $q = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C is the electron charge and $I$ is the DC current through the junction.

Shot noise is **white** (flat with frequency) and scales with DC bias current. In MOSFETs operating in strong inversion, shot noise is generally negligible compared to thermal and flicker noise. In BJTs and diodes it is a primary noise source.

### Worked Example

A BJT with $I_C = 1\,\text{mA}$ over a 10 MHz bandwidth:

$$I_{n,\text{rms}} = \sqrt{2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 10^{-3} \times 10^7} = \sqrt{3.2 \times 10^{-15}} \approx 56.6 \; \text{pA}$$

At $I_C = 1\,\text{mA}$, $g_m = I_C/V_T \approx 38.5\,\text{mA/V}$, so the input-referred noise voltage is:

$$V_{n,\text{in}} = \frac{I_{n,\text{rms}}}{g_m} = \frac{56.6\,\text{pA}}{38.5\,\text{mA/V}} \approx 1.47\,\text{nV}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$$

## Combining Noise Sources in Circuits

Noise sources are **uncorrelated** — they add in quadrature (sum of squares), not linearly:

$$S_{V,\text{total}} = S_{V,1} + S_{V,2} + S_{V,3} + \ldots$$

$$V_{n,\text{rms,total}} = \sqrt{V_{n,1}^2 + V_{n,2}^2 + V_{n,3}^2 + \ldots}$$

### Input-Referred Noise

All noise sources are referred to the input of the circuit for comparison and budgeting. For an amplifier with voltage gain $A_v$:

$$V_{n,\text{in}} = \frac{V_{n,\text{out}}}{A_v}$$

### Worked Example: Common-Source Amplifier

A common-source amplifier with load resistor $R_D$ has two dominant noise contributions:

1. **Channel thermal noise** of the MOSFET: $\overline{i_{n,d}^2} = 4k_BT\gamma g_m$ where $\gamma \approx 2/3$ in saturation
2. **Thermal noise of $R_D$**: $\overline{v_{n,R_D}^2} = 4k_BTR_D$

Output noise PSD:

$$S_{V,\text{out}} = 4k_BT\gamma g_m R_D^2 + 4k_BTR_D$$

Input-referred noise PSD (divide by $A_v^2 = g_m^2 R_D^2$):

$$S_{V,\text{in}} = \frac{4k_BT\gamma}{g_m} + \frac{4k_BT}{g_m^2 R_D}$$

To minimize input-referred noise: maximize $g_m$ (higher bias current or larger $W/L$) and use a large $R_D$ so the second term becomes negligible.

## Noise Simulation in Cadence Virtuoso

### SP (Small-Signal Noise) Analysis

SP noise analysis is the standard starting point for linear circuits — amplifiers, filters, LNAs. It computes noise spectral density at a specified output node and refers it to the input.

Setup in ADE:
1. Add an **SP** analysis and enable **Noise**
2. Set **Output port** to your output node, **Input port** to your source
3. Set the frequency sweep range (e.g. 1 Hz to 1 GHz for a wideband amplifier)
4. Run and plot `out.noisein` for input-referred noise [V/√Hz]

To see which devices dominate: Results → Direct Plot → Noise Summary. This ranks contributors by percentage and is the fastest way to identify what to optimize.

### PSS + PNoise (Periodic Circuits)

For circuits with a periodic large-signal operating point — PLLs, switched-capacitor filters, mixers, VCOs — use PSS followed by PNoise:

1. **PSS**: establishes the periodic steady state at the clock or carrier frequency
2. **PNoise**: computes noise folding and upconversion at offset frequencies around the harmonics

This is essential for VCO phase noise, charge-pump noise in PLLs, and kT/C noise in switched-capacitor circuits — where noise aliases from high frequencies back to baseband on each clock edge and SP analysis gives incorrect results.

### Reading Results

- **Spot noise**: noise spectral density at a single frequency [V²/Hz or V/√Hz]
- **Integrated noise**: total RMS noise over a bandwidth — in ADE Calculator, use `integ()` on the noise density curve over your signal bandwidth
- **Noise figure**: available directly as `nf` in SP results for RF circuits; $\text{NF} = 10\log_{10}(F)$ where $F$ is the noise factor
- **Noise contributors**: Results → Noise Summary — sorted by contribution percentage; use this before any sizing iteration

### Practical Tips

- Run noise simulation across process corners (SS, TT, FF) — the flicker noise coefficient $K$ is strongly process-dependent
- For a fully differential amplifier, set the input port to the differential pair, not a single-ended node — single-ended referral halves the apparent gain and doubles the apparent noise
- The noise bandwidth of a single-pole system is $B_n = \frac{\pi}{2} f_{-3\text{dB}}$, not $f_{-3\text{dB}}$ — use this when cross-checking simulated integrated noise against hand calculations
